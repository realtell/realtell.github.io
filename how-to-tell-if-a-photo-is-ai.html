How to Tell if a Photo Is AI-Generated: 10 Practical Checks

AI images have become so realistic that a quick glance is no longer enough. The good news is that you don't need to rely on your eyes alone. Here is a step-by-step routine that combines file evidence, source checking and visual clues.

Start with the evidence inside the file

1. Check for Content Credentials and AI metadata

ChatGPT, Sora, Google Gemini and Adobe Firefly attach Content Credentials to the files they create. Stable Diffusion tools often save the prompt. Drop the file into RealTell and you will see in seconds whether any of these labels are present. If one is found, you have your strongest possible answer.

2. Look for camera data

Photos straight from a phone or camera usually include the device model, lens, exposure settings and the time the picture was taken. AI images don't have these – unless someone copied them in. Camera data is good supporting evidence, not proof on its own.

3. Notice when there is no metadata at all

A file with no metadata has usually been screenshotted or passed through a social app. That isn't suspicious by itself, but it means you need the next steps.

Check where the image came from

4. Run a reverse image search

Upload the image to Google Lens or TinEye. Look for the earliest copy: was it first posted by a news agency, a photographer, or an AI art account? Real news photos usually appear on several reputable sites with a credited photographer.

5. Check the account and the context

Who posted it first? Does the account regularly share AI art? Is the image tied to an event that other reliable sources report? A dramatic photo of a “breaking” event that no news outlet has published deserves suspicion.

Then look closely at the picture

6. Hands, teeth and ears

Modern generators handle hands much better than they used to, but extra or merged fingers, irregular teeth and mismatched earrings still appear, especially in busy scenes.

7. Text and logos

Signs, labels, licence plates and shirts often contain letters that look right from a distance but turn into nonsense when you zoom in.

8. Background logic

Check railings, windows, fences and tiles. AI often produces patterns that drift, lines that don't connect, or people in the background with distorted faces.

9. Light, shadows and reflections

Are shadows pointing in a consistent direction? Do mirrors and glasses reflect what should be there? Physics errors are some of the most reliable visual clues.

10. The “too perfect” look

Flawless skin, perfect symmetry, dreamy lighting and an unusually cinematic composition are typical of AI images. Real photos usually contain small imperfections – noise, motion blur, cluttered edges.

Remember: no single check is conclusive. Combine evidence from the file, the source and the picture itself before deciding – and be careful about accusing anyone based on a hunch.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most reliable way to tell if a photo is AI?

A Content Credentials label from the tool that created it. When that is missing, combine a reverse image search with careful visual checks.

Are AI image detectors accurate?

Metadata checks are very reliable when a label is present. Tools that guess from pixels make frequent mistakes, including flagging real photos.

Can AI images have camera metadata?

Yes, metadata can be copied or faked, so camera data is supporting evidence rather than proof.
